# Voice profile: Lenny Ramirez

Built from one full sample the writer confirmed as their own and as having passed AI detection: "Getting Out of the Garden," an MLA-format college essay for English 10133 (about 1,500 words, 9 paragraphs) comparing *The Truman Show* to Genesis. Use this profile when Lenny asks for a rewrite "in my voice," for school essays, or for any text that should sound like them. A newer sample from Lenny overrides anything here.

The short version: **an academic essay written the way a smart student talks it through out loud.** Formal structure (MLA heading, works cited, quotes with citations, a clear argument) with spoken-register sentences inside it. No dashes, no semicolons, no stock transitions, a few casual hedges, and first-person comments placed exactly where the argument turns.

## The habits, with the writer's own examples

### 1. Spoken-register word choice inside a formal essay

Plain, everyday nouns and verbs where an AI would reach for Latinate ones.

- "the guy who created the show" (not "the show's creator/architect")
- "his dad" (not "his father"); "treated like a kid forever"
- "God ends up kicking them both out for good" (not "expels them")
- "Christof wanted to write his dad out of the show"
- "she gets pulled off the set and taken off the show"
- "a giant set under a dome"
- "That line hurts because it's true."

**Phrasal verbs are a signature.** goes after, ends up, piecing it together, talk him into, write out, kicking out, pulled off, gets flipped around, switches that around. When a draft says *expel, attempt, persuade, reveal, discover, eliminate*, try the phrasal verb Lenny would say.

**"ends up" construction:** "God ends up kicking them both out," "his fear ends up doing the same job as the flaming sword." Use it at most once or twice per essay.

**get-passive instead of be-passive:** "gets pulled off," "gets forced out," "getting paid," "gets his lines fed to him," "getting sent away." When a passive is needed, use *get*, not *is/was*.

### 2. Casual hedges and intensifiers, used lightly

pretty much, pretty obviously, pretty clear, really, just (7 times), actually (5 times), basically, literally, kind of, totally, exactly, a lot stronger.

These are the writer's voice, not filler. Keep them at roughly this rate, about one per 100 words. Do not stack two in one sentence. Do not remove them under §7 ("Actually" is on that list) or §24 (qualifiers). This writer's sample overrides both sections.

### 3. First person marks the turns in the argument

The writer never says "I will argue" or "In this essay." First person shows up as a report of what reading or watching was like, at the point where the essay moves forward:

- "That scene reminded me of the first chapter of Genesis..."
- "The part I found most interesting is that Weir takes all of these and changes what they mean." (This is the thesis.)
- "Knowing Genesis changed how I saw this ending."
- "The more I thought about it, the more his speech sounded like what the serpent said, just backwards."
- "So by the end I wasn't really asking whether Truman should leave. I was wondering if..."
- "I think that argument gets settled when Truman walks out the door..."

Put the thesis in a plain "The part I found most interesting is that..." sentence. Use one first-person reaction per body section at most.

### 4. Sentences lean on the one before them

Many sentences make no sense on their own, which is §36 working in the writer's favor:

- "That wasn't random."
- "Christof can't do that."
- "It's the other way around for Truman."
- "But the movie doesn't treat her like that."
- "That penalty is exactly what the serpent goes after..."
- "So the person who made this world literally lives up in its sky."

**Openers that point backward or forward in sequence:** The, In, Then (4), That (4), So (3), If (3), But (2). "So" begins a sentence that draws the conclusion from the previous one. "Then" moves the plot along.

### 5. Quote, then say it in plain words

Every quotation is followed by a short, plain restatement or by what follows from it. The writer does not analyze the quote's language. They say what it means for the story.

> ...God "placed at the east of the garden of Eden Cherubims, and a flaming sword which turned every way, to keep the way of the tree of life" (Gen. 3.24). The angels are there to keep people from getting back in.

Quotes are introduced mid-sentence or with a colon. They are never dropped in as standalone sentences.

### 6. Topic sentences are plain, slightly clunky signposts

- "One example is the way each garden guards its border."
- "Sylvia is another thing that gets flipped around."
- "The biggest change is at the end of the movie."
- "Truman's answer is what finishes flipping the story."
- "The ending isn't totally happy, though."

Use the pattern *[thing] is [another / the biggest / what...]*. Do not write polished "X further illustrates Y" sentences. Sentence-final "though" for a turn is a nice touch. Use it once.

### 7. The "In X... / The movie..." comparison frame

The writer opens many sentences with "In Eden," "In Genesis," or "In Seahaven" (7 times). In a comparison essay this frame is theirs, so §37 does not apply here. What keeps it human is that the second half changes every time: "It's the other way around for Truman." "Christof can't do that." "The movie sees it the opposite way, where..." Keep the frame, and vary the reply.

The writer names the central idea (reversal) with plain, varied words: flipped, the opposite way, the other way around, switches that around, just backwards, except it keeps Truman in instead of keeping him out. Names stay fixed (Truman, Christof, Sylvia, Seahaven, Eden, Genesis). Only the "flip" language varies.

### 8. Relaxed commas

The writer usually skips the comma after a short opening phrase or clause and between short joined clauses:

- "In Eden God gave the first man one rule..."
- "If you don't know Genesis the ending is just a satisfying escape..."
- "If anything it's the first time he gets to be his own person."
- "His wife is getting paid to be married to him and his best friend gets his lines fed to him..."

Keep this. Do not "correct" it. Put commas back only where a sentence would be misread without one.

### 9. Lists get broken up, not tripled

A five-part parallel is split across two sentences instead of written as one neat list: "Both have a closed-off paradise and a creator who gives the man everything he needs. There's also a rule he isn't supposed to break, an outsider who tells him the truth, and a way out." Sometimes items are chained with repeated *and*: "being safe and fed and treated like a kid forever." Avoid tidy groups of three made of matching abstract nouns.

### 10. Concrete specifics over abstractions

The writer shows things with plot events, not labels: "First a stage light falls out of the sky, and later his car radio starts describing exactly where he's driving. Then he sees his dad, who supposedly drowned in front of him when he was a kid, just standing on the street." Use *First... later... Then...* for a series of events.

### 11. Opening and ending

- **Opening:** Start in the story with a plain, surprising fact ("Nobody tells Truman Burbank... that a TV company adopted him as a baby"). Summarize the plot in present tense and close on "From there the movie is Truman slowly piecing it together and then trying to get out." No hook question, no broad claim about society or literature.
- **Ending:** Admit a complication first ("The ending isn't totally happy, though."). Then reframe the question ("I wasn't really asking X. I was wondering if Y."). End on one specific observation, hedged with "I think" and "basically" ("his name is basically 'True Man' and that's the moment he actually becomes one"). Never end with a send-off about lasting relevance (§25).

### 12. Claims stay modest and specific

The writer judges with plain comparatives, not big claims: "that makes the movie's criticism of him a lot stronger," "the movie makes it pretty clear," "he doesn't really try to hide it." Never "profound," "powerful commentary," "timeless," or "masterfully."

## Things this writer never does

Em or en dashes. Semicolons. Rhetorical questions. Parenthetical asides. "However," "Moreover," "Additionally," "Furthermore," "Thus," "In conclusion." "This essay will..." Abstract nouns as sentence subjects ("The juxtaposition of..."). Fragments for drama. Bold or headings inside the essay. Words from §7 other than "actually." Ending on a moral about society.

## Before and after

AI-style paragraph on the same material (no new facts):

> The film's climactic confrontation serves as a powerful inversion of the Genesis narrative. Rather than expelling Truman, Christof attempts to persuade him to remain — highlighting the fundamental difference between divine authority and manipulative control. Moreover, Christof's assertion that Truman has "nothing to fear" underscores the film's critique of comfortable ignorance, ultimately positioning Truman's departure as a triumphant act of self-determination.

In Lenny's voice:

> The biggest change is at the end. In Genesis God doesn't negotiate with Adam at all. He just drives him out. Christof can't do that, so he has to try and talk Truman into staying, and the more I thought about it the more his speech sounded like the serpent's, just backwards. He tells Truman that in his world he has "nothing to fear" (The Truman Show), which is basically saying that not knowing things is what keeps him safe. So when Truman walks out anyway, it's the first thing he's ever done that was actually his choice.

What changed: plain topic sentence, the "In Genesis... / Christof can't do that" frame, a phrasal verb (talk into), a first-person reading reaction at the turn, a quote followed by a plain restatement, "So" to draw the conclusion, two light hedges (basically, actually), contractions, no dash, no "Moreover," and no "triumphant act of self-determination."

## Quick checklist for a rewrite in this voice

1. Dashes and semicolons: zero.
2. Contractions: about 3 per 100 words.
3. Hedges (pretty, just, really, actually, basically, kind of): about 1 per 100 words, never two in one sentence.
4. At least one sentence per paragraph that only makes sense because of the one before it ("That wasn't random." / "Christof can't do that.").
5. Every quote is followed by a plain-words restatement.
6. Topic sentences are plain signposts ("X is another thing that...", "The biggest change is...").
7. First person only at the turns in the argument, as a reading reaction.
8. Phrasal verbs and get-passives instead of Latinate verbs and be-passives.
9. Skip the comma after short openers like "In Eden" or "If you don't know Genesis."
10. Mostly 15–30 word sentences joined with and/so/since/where, with a few short ones. Don't split long sentences to look "simpler."
11. Before turning it in, check that the rewrite adds no facts, quotes, or citations the writer didn't provide.
